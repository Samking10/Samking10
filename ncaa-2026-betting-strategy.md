## 1. Where the season stands

Indiana enters as defending champion after a 16–0 season capped by a 27–21 win over Miami
in the CFP National Championship — the program's first title in 139 years. The Hoosiers
return as a top-five team but lost a Heisman-winning quarterback, which is why the market
has them behind Ohio State rather than ahead of it.

This is a high-turnover offseason league-wide: Lane Kiffin has moved from Ole Miss to LSU,
LSU is fielding a near-entirely new roster, and Texas Tech is working through a quarterback
change. Seven schools sit at 11-1 or shorter. That is a wide, unsettled top tier.

Only three teams rank top-five in **both** offensive and defensive SP+: Ohio State, Oregon,

**The case against, stated honestly.** Ohio State draws the No. 4 toughest schedule by SP+,

is roughly an 85% chance the champion is someone other than Ohio State. A "prediction" here
is a plurality pick, not a likely event. Treat it accordingly.

**Runner-up if you want one team to beat them: Oregon.** SP+ No. 2, top-five on both sides
of the ball, priced fifth at +800.

## 4. The stop-loss math you need to see first

At a standard -110 price, a $10 bet risks $10 to win $9.09. **Break-even win rate is 52.4%.**

A $40 stop loss against $10 units gives you a **four-unit leash**. Here is what that means:

| Your true win rate | Approx. chance of hitting -$40 at some point |
|---|---|
| 52.4% (break-even) | ~100% given enough bets |
| 53% | ~62% |
| 55% (elite handicapper) | ~45% |

*(Gambler's-ruin approximation; the true numbers are slightly worse because -110 pays less
than even money.)*

Read the top row again. A break-even bettor hits a four-unit drawdown *eventually with
certainty* — a random walk with no drift always reaches -4 if you keep walking. Even at 55%,
a rate almost no one sustains across a season, you bust roughly two times in five.

**This is the real problem with the plan as written, and it is fixable.** $10 against a $40
stop is 25% of bankroll per bet, where conventional sizing is 1–2%. You have two coherent
options:

- **Option A — keep the $10 unit.** Then accept that this is a *short, deliberate* run of
  maybe 8–12 bets, not a season-long program. Be extremely selective. This is the version
  described below.
- **Option B — keep betting all season.** Then the unit has to drop to **$2–$4**, giving you
  10–20 units of room instead of 4.

Option A is what you asked for, so that is what follows. But add a **stop-win**: without one,
a stop-loss-only plan has exactly one terminal state, and it is the loss.

## 5. The plan (Option A)

**Bankroll:** $40 risk capital. **Unit:** $10. **Season ends at -$40 or +$40, whichever first.**

| Rule | Detail |
|---|---|
| Max exposure | 2 bets per weekend, never more than $20 live at once |
| Bet types | Point spreads and totals only, priced -105 to -120 |
| Moneyline cap | Nothing shorter than -200. No exceptions |
| Parlays | None. A 3-leg parlay at -110 legs wins ~12.5% of the time |
| After a loss | Same $10 next time. Never increase to recover |
| Stop-win | At +$40, bank $20 permanently and play the rest on the original $40 |
| Hard stop | -$40 cumulative for the season. Walk. Do not reload |
| Week 1 | **Maximum 2 bets.** See below |

**Why cap Week 1.** Week 1 is the least predictable week of the year: no current-season data
exists, coaching staffs are new, and portal turnover is at its peak this offseason
specifically. Spending half your four-unit leash on the noisiest week of the season is the
worst available use of it. Lines get sharper — and your reads get better — from Week 3 on.

## 6. Highest-probability payouts, Week 1 (Sept 3–7)

You asked which matchups have the highest probability of paying out. That question has two
different answers, and separating them matters.

### Tier A — Highest raw win probability, but do not bet these

| Game | Date | Reality |
|---|---|---|
| Ball State at Ohio State | Sept 5, 12:30 ET | ~99% win probability. ML around -20000+ |
| Kent State at South Carolina | Sept 5, 12:45 ET | Same shape |
| Western Michigan at Michigan | Sept 5, 7:30 ET | Same shape |

These are the highest-probability outcomes on the board and they are **traps**. At -2000,
$10 returns about $0.50. You need roughly 40 straight wins to gain one unit, and a single
upset erases all of it. This is the most common way a small bankroll dies. The Moneyline cap
rule above exists specifically to keep you out of these.

### Tier B — Best probability-to-price balance (the actual candidates)

## 7. Honest summary

- **Champion pick: Ohio State.** Best team by the leading public model and by the market. Also
  only ~15% likely — the pick is a plurality, not a prediction anyone should lean on.
- **Highest-probability payouts** are the Week 1 cupcake moneylines, and they are unbettable
  at their prices. The best *bettable* probability is Oregon over Boise State.
- **The strategy's binding constraint is the stop loss, not the picks.** Four units of room
  means selectivity is worth more than handicapping skill. Two good bets beat six decent ones.
- **Every bet described here is negative expected value** unless you genuinely beat the closing
  line, which most bettors do not. The plan above is built to lose slowly and cap the damage
  at $40, which is what a stop loss is for. It is not built to make money, because at these
  prices and this bankroll size, nothing reliably is.
